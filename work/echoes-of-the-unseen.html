An XR experience exploring spatial memory through layered sound and volumetric imagery — asking how a place holds what has passed through it.

Developed for the Malta Arts Festival 2025, Echoes of the Unseen places visitors inside a reconstructed soundscape of a site that no longer exists in its original form. Wearing a lightweight headset, they move through an environment assembled from field recordings, volumetric captures, and archival photographic data — a space that is simultaneously familiar and dissolved.

Concept

Memory as Spatial Residue

The work departs from a phenomenological question: if memory is always a reconstruction, can a place be said to remember? Drawing on the writings of Gaston Bachelard and Mark Fisher's concept of hauntology, the piece constructs a space that is neither documentation nor fiction — it occupies the threshold between the two.

Sound is the primary carrier of memory here. Binaural field recordings layered with synthesis create a sonic architecture that shifts as the visitor moves, so that what is heard depends entirely on where one stands. The visual layer — volumetric figures rendered from photogrammetry — appears at the edge of legibility, never quite resolving into sharp form.

Process

Volumetric Capture & Spatial Audio Design

Production began with a series of field recording sessions across heritage sites in Malta, capturing ambience, resonance, and human presence. These recordings were then spatialised using Resonance Audio within Unity, with each sound source anchored to a specific spatial coordinate in the virtual environment.

Volumetric figures were generated through a photogrammetry pipeline — subjects photographed in structured-light sessions, reconstructed as point clouds, and imported into Unity with custom HLSL shaders to preserve the fragmented, particulate quality of the captures rather than converting them into clean meshes. The intentional roughness became an aesthetic principle: incompleteness as truth.

Context

Reception & Exhibition

Audiences at the Malta Arts Festival engaged with the piece in sessions of twelve minutes, navigating the space individually. Post-experience conversations consistently returned to the sensation of recognising something that couldn't be named — a response that confirmed the work's central proposition about memory's ambiguity.
