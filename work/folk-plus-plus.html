Algorithmically remixed folk music traditions, cross-referencing melodic and rhythmic pattern libraries across five cultures — discovering what folk music would sound like if traditions had been allowed to meet.

Folk++ begins from a counterfactual: what if the isolation that produces regional folk traditions had never existed? What if the melodic patterns of Maltese għana had been able to cross-pollinate with Sardinian cantu a tenore, Georgian polyphony, Northern Moroccan Gnawa, and the Appalachian shape-note tradition? The work constructs this imaginary encounter computationally, extracting pattern libraries from each tradition and generating new music from their intersection.

Concept

Algorithmic Ethnomusicology

The project proposes a methodology it calls algorithmic ethnomusicology: the use of computational pattern analysis to identify structural features of musical traditions — scale preferences, rhythmic templates, ornamentation vocabularies, call-and-response structures — and then to generate new material that honours these features while recombining them across cultural boundaries that would historically have prevented contact.

The premise is not that these traditions should be merged, but that their potential encounter reveals something about each of them that cannot be seen from inside. A melodic contour that is unremarkable within its own tradition becomes striking when it meets a rhythmic frame from four thousand kilometres away. Folk++ makes these encounters audible.

Process

Pattern Extraction & Generative Composition

Field recordings from each tradition were transcribed and annotated in collaboration with ethnomusicologists from the University of Malta and the Scuola di Musica di Fiesole. A custom Python analysis pipeline extracted structural features from each corpus — intervals, phrase lengths, ornamentation timing, modal preferences — building a formal grammar for each tradition.

The generative composition engine draws from these grammars simultaneously, weighting cross-cultural combinations by a parameter called "encounter distance" — the estimated probability that the two traditions would historically have come into contact. Low-distance encounters (e.g., Maltese and Moroccan, given centuries of maritime trade) produce more coherent hybrids; high-distance encounters (e.g., Georgian and Appalachian) produce more radical juxtapositions. The result is an hour-long generative piece that never repeats.

The live performance version — presented at Ars Electronica and CTM Festival — adds a performer interpreting the generated material in real time, their instrument choice determined by random selection from the five source traditions.

Context

Reception & Scholarly Response

The work received significant attention from the ethnomusicology community, with the methodology paper published in the proceedings of the International Council for Traditional Music. At Ars Electronica, it was awarded an Honorary Mention in the Digital Musics & Sound Art category. A full-length recording of the generative piece is available as a limited edition 2xLP.
